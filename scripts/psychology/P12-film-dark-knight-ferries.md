# P12: The Joker's ferry experiment was wrong (and the movie knew it)

- **Category:** Film
- **Concepts:** the "panic myth" in disasters, emergent shared identity, attitudes vs. behavior (LaPiere)
- **Target length:** ~4:00 (~590 words) · regular video
- **Title:** The Joker's social experiment, tested against real disasters
- **Thumbnail text:** "WOULD YOU PRESS IT?"

---

**COLD OPEN**
In *The Dark Knight*, the Joker runs a social experiment. Two ferries, both rigged with bombs. One full of regular citizens, one full of prisoners. Each boat holds the detonator for the *other* boat. If nobody presses it by midnight, he blows up both. The Joker's whole philosophy is that when things get bad, people turn on each other. Civilization is a thin coat of paint. So, was he right? We actually have decades of real data on how people behave in disasters, and the answer surprised a lot of researchers.

**1. THE PANIC MYTH**
Sociologist Enrico Quarantelli spent about fifty years studying real disasters: floods, fires, explosions, hundreds of field studies. Over and over, he found almost no real panic. No stampedes of people trampling each other to survive. What he found instead was people helping. The idea that crowds go feral in a crisis is so common, and so wrong, that researchers call it the panic myth.

**2. WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY DID**
On September 11th, survivors of the World Trade Center described a mostly calm, orderly evacuation, with strangers helping each other down the stairs. After the 2005 London bombings, psychologist John Drury and colleagues interviewed survivors from the attacked trains and found the same thing: people comforted strangers, shared water, helped the injured. Drury's explanation is that disasters create a sudden shared identity. A train car full of strangers becomes "us." The danger doesn't dissolve the group. It creates one.

**3. BUT THE CIVILIANS VOTED TO DO IT**
Here's where the movie gets really smart. On the civilian ferry, they hold a vote, and the majority votes to blow up the prisoners. On paper, the Joker wins. But when it's time to actually press the button, nobody can do it. And on the prisoner boat, a huge inmate takes the detonator and throws it out the window.

**4. WHAT WE SAY VS. WHAT WE DO**
That gap has a name in psychology, and the classic study on it is personal to me. In the early 1930s, sociologist Richard LaPiere traveled the US with a young Chinese couple, at a time when anti-Asian prejudice was open and normal. They visited more than 250 hotels and restaurants. They were refused service once. Then LaPiere mailed those same businesses a letter asking, "Will you accept members of the Chinese race as guests?" About nine out of ten who answered said no. What people say in the abstract and what they do face to face with a real human can be completely different. The civilians voted against an idea: "prisoners." They couldn't press the button on actual people.

**5. THE REAL MONSTER IN DISASTERS**
Disaster researchers have one more term worth knowing: "elite panic." Often the people who panic in a crisis are the ones in charge, because they *assume* the public will panic, then make decisions like withholding information. The Joker is the ultimate elite panicker. He's so sure people are monsters that he can't see the evidence in front of him.

**OUTRO**
The Joker said people are only as good as the world allows them to be. The research says the opposite: when the world falls apart, people usually get better. That's not naive. It's data. Would you have pressed the button? Be honest in the comments.
