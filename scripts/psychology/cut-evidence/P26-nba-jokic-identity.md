# P26: Nikola Jokić's secret weapon might be his horses

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video
- **Concepts:** self-complexity, athletic identity, action anticipation in experts
- **Target length:** ~3:45 (~580 words)
- **Title:** The psychology of not needing basketball
- **Thumbnail text:** "HE LEFT FOR A HORSE RACE"

---

**COLD OPEN**
June 2023. Nikola Jokić has just won his first NBA championship and Finals MVP. The city of Denver is throwing him a parade. And what he reportedly wanted to know was whether he'd make it home in time. For a horse race. Days later, he was in Sombor, Serbia, watching his family's horses run. He just got his 200th career triple-double, a mark only Russell Westbrook has reached. He might be the most casual genius in sports history. And psychology has a theory for why that casualness might not be a quirk. It might be the point.

**1. THE TACO BELL PICK**
Start at the beginning. The 2014 NBA Draft. Jokić goes 41st overall. It was such a non-event that ESPN was showing a Taco Bell commercial when his pick came through. He was reportedly asleep. He says he's never eaten Taco Bell because of it. Now he's one of the best players alive. So how does someone like that stay so level?

**2. DON'T PUT ALL YOUR EGGS IN ONE BASKET**
That's the actual title of a 1985 paper. Psychologist Patricia Linville came up with an idea called self-complexity. Picture your identity as a collection of parts: student, friend, brother, athlete, horse guy. If you've got just one or two parts, and that part fails, your whole self takes the hit. If you've got many separate parts, a disaster in one leaves the others standing. In her 1987 study, people with more complex selves reported less depression and less illness after stressful events. The later research is mixed, so don't treat it as a law. But the core idea has stuck around for 40 years.

**3. HERCULES OR ACHILLES?**
Sports psychology has a version with the greatest title ever. In 1993, Britton Brewer and his colleagues published "Athletic Identity: Hercules' Muscles or Achilles Heel?" Their point: seeing yourself as an athlete and nothing else is both. It can make you relentless. It can also make you fragile, because when you lose a game, or get hurt, you don't just lose a game. You lose you. Now think about Jokić. A bad night in Denver isn't a verdict on who he is. He's got a family, a hometown and a stable. That might be why he plays so loose, why he's happy to pass up a shot that a more anxious superstar would take to prove something.

**4. WHY HE SEES IT FIRST**
Of course, being relaxed doesn't make you great. So what does? Maybe this. In 2008, Italian researchers showed clips of free throws to elite basketball players, to coaches and sportswriters, and to complete novices. They cut the video off early. The players could predict whether a shot would go in earlier and more accurately than anyone, even before the ball left the shooter's hands. They were reading the body. Coaches and journalists, who'd watched just as much basketball, couldn't. Your own experience of doing a skill lets you see it differently. I'd bet that's part of what's happening when Jokić throws a pass to someone who doesn't know he's open yet.

**5. THE TRAP**
Now don't take the wrong lesson. Jokić isn't great because he doesn't care. You don't get to 200 triple-doubles by not caring. The lesson isn't "care less." It's "don't let one thing be all of you." You can love something completely without needing it to prove you exist.

**OUTRO**
So here's a question I'd like you to actually think about. If your main thing fell apart tomorrow, your job, your sport, your grades, who would you still be? Everybody needs a horse. What's yours? Tell me in the comments.
