# P15: Why politicians talk like 6th graders (and why it works on you)

- **Category:** Politics (**strictly nonpartisan**: examples from both parties, no policy opinions)
- **Concepts:** processing fluency, the illusory truth effect, rhyme-as-reason, readability
- **Target length:** ~3:45 (~560 words) · regular video · **timely: US midterms are Tuesday, Nov 3**
- **Title:** The psychology of political slogans: why simple wins
- **Thumbnail text:** "5TH GRADE LEVEL"

---

**COLD OPEN**
November 19th, 1863. Edward Everett, the most famous speaker in America, gives a speech at Gettysburg. It runs about two hours. Then Abraham Lincoln stands up and talks for about two minutes, and *that's* the one carved in stone. With elections on Tuesday, you're about to hear a lot of politicians talk. Here's the psychology of why the simplest lines win, and why your brain falls for them no matter which side you're on.

**1. THE GRADE-LEVEL STUDY**
In 2016, researchers at Carnegie Mellon ran presidential candidates' speeches through readability tests, the same tools used to grade school textbooks. Most candidates, from both parties, spoke at about a 6th to 8th grade level. Donald Trump's grammar came in just under 6th grade. Lincoln's Gettysburg Address? Around 11th grade. The study was small and not peer-reviewed, so don't overread it. But the pattern is real: modern campaign speech is simple, on purpose.

**2. EASY FEELS TRUE**
Why? Something psychologists call processing fluency. When information is easy for your brain to process, it doesn't just feel easier. It feels more *true*. In a 1999 study, Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz showed people statements printed in colors that were either easy or hard to read against the background. Same statements. People rated the easy-to-read ones as more likely to be true. Your brain mistakes "easy to process" for "probably correct."

**3. REPEAT IT UNTIL IT'S TRUE**
Add repetition. In 1977, researchers found that statements people heard more than once were rated as more believable, even when nobody checked whether they were true. It's called the illusory truth effect. Later studies found it works even on statements people *know* are false. That's why slogans get repeated a thousand times. "Yes we can." "Make America great again." "Lock her up." "Feel the Bern." Doesn't matter the side, the mechanism is the same.

**4. RHYMES ARE MORE BELIEVABLE**
And then there's rhyme. Researchers Matthew McGlone and Jessica Tofighbakhsh gave people sayings in two versions. One rhymed, like "woes unite foes." The other meant exactly the same thing but didn't rhyme: "woes unite enemies." People rated the rhyming version as more accurate. It's called the rhyme-as-reason effect. "I like Ike" wasn't just catchy. It was persuasive by design.

**5. SIMPLE ISN'T DUMB**
To be fair: speaking simply is a skill, not a sign of low intelligence. Lincoln was simple *and* brilliant. Clear language includes more people. The problem isn't simplicity. It's that simple, repeated, rhyming phrases can make us feel certain about things we've never actually thought through.

**OUTRO**
So this week, try one thing. When a line hits you and you think "yeah, exactly," pause and ask: do I agree with this, or does it just sound good? That one question is the best defense your brain has against every campaign, from every side. Go vote, if you can. And tell me the most effective slogan you've ever heard. Not the best policy. The most effective *slogan*.
