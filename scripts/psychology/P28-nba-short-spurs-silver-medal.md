# P28: Why second place hurts more than third

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** under 3 min, post as a YouTube Short too
- **Concepts:** counterfactual thinking, upward vs. downward comparison
- **Target length:** ~2:15 (~320 words)
- **Title:** Why silver medalists look sadder than bronze medalists
- **Thumbnail text:** "UP 29. LOST."

---

**HOOK**
The Spurs led Game 4 of the NBA Finals by 29 points. It was the biggest lead ever blown in a Finals game. They lost by one. And a long line of Olympic research suggests second place can hurt more than third.

**1. THE OLYMPIC STUDY**
In 1995, psychologists Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich watched TV footage from the 1992 Olympics and rated how happy medalists looked, from 1 to 10. Bronze medalists averaged 7.1. Silver medalists averaged 4.8. Let that sink in. The people who beat them looked less happy.

**2. WHY**
It comes down to what you compare yourself to. Psychologists call it counterfactual thinking: imagining what almost happened. The silver medalist looks up: "I almost won gold." The bronze medalist looks down: "I almost got nothing." Third place was close to no medal. Second place was close to everything.

**3. IT HELD UP**
Different teams have found the same pattern: a 2005 study, a 2006 study of judo photos, and a 2021 study that used software to read the faces of 413 athletes from five Olympic Games. They've added a detail: silver medalists often expected more going in, so the gap between hope and reality was bigger.

**4. THE SPURS**
Now think about San Antonio. They beat the Thunder in the Western Conference Finals. Everybody ranked below them gets "at least we made the playoffs." The Spurs get "if only." If only they'd held 29 points. If only that one game had gone differently. That's about the most painful "if only" a team could have. This research is on Olympians, not NBA teams, but the logic carries over.

**ENDING**
But "if only" isn't just pain. Researchers have found it can be fuel, because it shows you exactly what to change next time. So if you came in second at something, the job you almost got, the person who almost said yes, it hurts because you were close. That's what the hurt means. You were close. What's your biggest "if only"?
