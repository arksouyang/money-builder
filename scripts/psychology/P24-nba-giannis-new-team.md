# P24: Why Giannis's Heat started 0-4 (and what 14 NBA seasons show)

- **Title:** Why new superteams always look terrible

**COLD OPEN**
Miami finally got its superstar. Giannis Antetokounmpo, a two-time MVP, arrives with the most famous team culture in basketball. First game, he scores 35. The crowd's chanting MVP within two minutes. And they lose. Then they lose again. And again. Four straight, the franchise's worst start since 2007, and the first time Giannis has ever lost his first four. Here's the weird part. He was averaging 28 points on 55 percent shooting. So talent wasn't the problem. To see what was, we have to look at 14 seasons of NBA data.

**1. WHAT THE NBA DATA SHOWS**
In 2002, researchers Shawn Berman, Jonathan Down and Charles Hill studied 23 NBA teams across 14 seasons. They measured how much experience each team's players had together. Teams with more shared experience performed better, even after controlling for things like age, draft position and coaching experience. Their argument: teams build tacit knowledge, the stuff players know but can't really write down. And the University of Washington reported that losing teams that added shared experience won about 5.7 more games the following year. There's a catch. The benefit levels off, and it can eventually turn negative if a group stays together too long. But early on, it's real.

**2. THE MAP IN THE TEAM'S HEAD**
Why would that matter? Psychologist Daniel Wegner came up with a concept called transactive memory. Think of a couple. One remembers birthdays, the other remembers the passwords. Neither one holds everything, but the pair does, because each one knows who knows what. Teams work like that too. And it's been tested. In 1995, researchers taught people to build an AM radio. Some were trained individually. Some were trained as a group. Same instruction. When it was time to build, the groups trained together remembered more and made fewer mistakes. They didn't know more about radios. They knew more about each other. A meta-analysis of 65 studies, updated in 2017, found that this kind of shared team knowledge predicts performance, even after accounting for how motivated a team is and how well it works together.

**3. WHAT THAT LOOKS LIKE ON THE FLOOR**
On a basketball court, that map is everything. Who rotates when I get beat? Does he want the ball early or late? If I leave my man, is someone coming? That can't be downloaded in training camp. And here's the hidden cost of a blockbuster trade. Miami didn't just add Giannis and Bobby Portis. It sent away Tyler Herro, Kel'el Ware, Jaime Jaquez and Kasparas Jakucionis. That's not just talent going out the door. That's pages being torn out of the map. In an early loss, Minnesota put up 131 on them.

**4. WHY WE BLAME THE STAR ANYWAY**
So why did all the talk go straight to Giannis? Psychologists call it the fundamental attribution error. When something goes wrong, we blame the person and underrate the situation. A star's name is easy to see. A missing rotation isn't. Even Giannis took responsibility himself. But it's the same thing we do when a new coworker struggles in week one. We say they're not that good, when really they just don't know where anything is yet.

**5. THE BORING FIX**
[UPDATE BEFORE FILMING: They were 1-4 after beating Atlanta on Oct 28.] Then came the first win: 24 points and 18 rebounds for Giannis, while Bam Adebayo became the franchise's all-time leading rebounder. One win doesn't fix anything. But the research points to a boring answer. You can't buy shared knowledge. You can only build it, one possession at a time. And the NBA data measures this in seasons, not weeks. So I'd be very careful judging this team on five games.

**OUTRO**
So the next time you join a new team, a new job, a group project, give yourself a break. You're not bad at this. You're on day one. And the same goes for the new person sitting next to you. Tell me: what's the worst "day one" you've ever had?
