# P24: Why Giannis's Heat started 0-4 (and what pilots know about it)

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video · **update record before filming**
- **Concepts:** team familiarity, transactive memory, fundamental attribution error
- **Target length:** ~3:45 (~560 words)
- **Title:** Why new superteams always look terrible
- **Thumbnail text:** "DAY ONE"

---

**COLD OPEN**
Miami finally got its superstar. Giannis Antetokounmpo, a two-time MVP, arrives with the most famous team culture in basketball. First game, he scores 35. The crowd's chanting MVP within two minutes. And they lose. Then they lose again. And again. Four straight, the franchise's worst start since 2007, and the first time Giannis has ever lost his first four. Here's the weird part. He was averaging 28 points on 55 percent shooting. So talent wasn't the problem. To see what was, we have to look at airplanes.

**1. THE MOST DANGEROUS DAY IN THE COCKPIT**
The National Transportation Safety Board once reviewed 37 serious US airline accidents. In 15 of them, investigators knew how long the pilots had worked together. In 11 of those 15, 73 percent, it was the crew's very first day flying together. That's a small sample, so it isn't proof. But the idea got tested. Harvard's Richard Hackman wrote about simulator research where crews who'd flown together for days made fewer errors than freshly assembled crews, even when they were tired. Same training. Same licenses. Different history.

**2. THE MAP IN THE TEAM'S HEAD**
Why would that matter? Psychologist Daniel Wegner came up with a concept called transactive memory. Think of a couple. One remembers birthdays, the other remembers the passwords. Neither one holds everything, but the pair does, because each one knows who knows what. Teams work like that too. In 1995, researchers taught people to build an AM radio. Some were trained individually. Some were trained as a group. Same instruction. When it was time to build, the groups trained together remembered more and made fewer mistakes. They didn't know more about radios. They knew more about each other.

**3. WHAT THAT LOOKS LIKE ON THE FLOOR**
On a basketball court, that map is everything. Who rotates when I get beat? Does he want the ball early or late? If I leave my man, is someone coming? That can't be downloaded in training camp. And here's the hidden cost of a blockbuster trade. Miami didn't just add Giannis and Bobby Portis. It sent away Tyler Herro, Kel'el Ware, Jaime Jaquez and Kasparas Jakucionis. That's not just talent going out the door. That's pages being torn out of the map. In an early loss, Minnesota put up 131 on them.

**4. WHY WE BLAME THE STAR ANYWAY**
So why did all the talk go straight to Giannis? Psychologists call it the fundamental attribution error. When something goes wrong, we blame the person and underrate the situation. A star's name is easy to see. A missing rotation isn't. Even Giannis took responsibility himself. But it's the same thing we do when a new coworker struggles in week one. We say they're not that good, when really they just don't know where anything is yet.

**5. THE BORING FIX**
[UPDATE BEFORE FILMING: They were 1-4 after beating Atlanta on Oct 28.] Then came the first win: 24 points and 18 rebounds for Giannis, while Bam Adebayo became the franchise's all-time leading rebounder. One win doesn't fix anything. But the research points to a boring answer. You can't buy shared memory. You can only build it, one possession at a time. If I were judging this team, I wouldn't do it until about 20 games in. That's my estimate, not a law.

**OUTRO**
So the next time you join a new team, a new job, a group project, give yourself a break. You're not bad at this. You're on day one. And the same goes for the new person sitting next to you. Tell me: what's the worst "day one" you've ever had?
