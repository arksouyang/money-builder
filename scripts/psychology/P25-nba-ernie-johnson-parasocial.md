# P25: Why it hurts that Ernie Johnson is leaving (even if you've never met him)

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video
- **Concepts:** parasocial relationships, parasocial breakups, socioemotional selectivity theory
- **Target length:** ~3:30 (~530 words)
- **Title:** Why we grieve people we've never met
- **Thumbnail text:** "HE DOESN'T KNOW ME"

---

**COLD OPEN**
In late October, a 70-year-old man posted a video saying he's leaving his job after this season. Charles Barkley teared up on air. People all over the internet talked like they were losing a family member. The man is Ernie Johnson, host of *Inside the NBA* for 37 seasons. And most of the people grieving have never met him. He has no idea they exist. So why does it hurt? Psychologists gave that feeling a name 70 years ago.

**1. INTIMACY AT A DISTANCE**
In 1956, sociologists Donald Horton and Richard Wohl noticed something about television. A TV host talks right into the camera, like he's talking to you, in your house, in your living room, at the same time every week. Your brain knows that isn't a relationship. It responds like it is one anyway. They called it parasocial interaction, or intimacy at a distance. It's one-sided. And *Inside the NBA* might be the perfect machine for it. Four people who clearly like each other, messing up, laughing, roasting each other, for decades. Ernie's the one holding it together. If you grew up on that show, he's been in your house more than some of your relatives.

**2. YOU'RE NOT DELUSIONAL**
"Parasocial" gets used like an insult now. It shouldn't be. Most people know exactly what these relationships are. In 2004, communication researcher Jonathan Cohen asked 381 adults how they'd feel if their favorite TV character went away. The stronger the bond, the worse the reaction they expected. People with anxious attachment styles, the ones who worry most in real relationships, expected it to hit hardest. Earlier work found viewers expected a reaction a lot like an actual breakup. That's not because they're confused. It's because the same system that bonds you to real people doesn't check for a screen.

**3. "IT'S TIME"**
So why's he leaving? Johnson said it was totally his decision. He's dealt with heart issues over the last two years. And he made a point of saying he's stepping back, not disappearing. He's still doing March Madness. Stanford psychologist Laura Carstensen has a theory that fits this. It's called socioemotional selectivity theory. When your future feels wide open, you collect things: new people, new goals, new experiences. When time feels limited, you get choosy, and you move toward what actually matters. It isn't really about age. When young people are told to imagine moving across the country, they start choosing their closest people too. I can't speak for Ernie Johnson. But "it's time" might be one of the healthiest sentences a person can say.

**4. THE ONE THAT ISN'T PARASOCIAL**
And then there's Barkley. He said over the summer that if Ernie goes, he goes. That's the part that gets me. We're grieving something one-sided. He's losing something real. Two men, decades of friendship, on camera. There aren't many places where you get to watch that. Maybe that's what we're actually going to miss.

**OUTRO**
So if a stranger leaving TV hits you harder than you expected, don't be embarrassed. Your brain did what it's supposed to do. Someone showed up, over and over, for years, and you got attached.
