# P27: The No. 1 pick might be a curse

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video · **update rookie stats before filming**
- **Concepts:** the loser's curse, overconfidence, regression to the mean, false consensus
- **Target length:** ~3:45 (~570 words)
- **Title:** Why smart people keep getting the draft wrong
- **Thumbnail text:** "THE LOSER'S CURSE"

---

**COLD OPEN**
Every June, NBA teams bet their futures on teenagers. This year's No. 1 pick, AJ Dybantsa, sprained his ankle and missed the first four games. In his debut, he scored 19. The No. 3 pick, Cameron Boozer, leads the league's first Rookie Ladder. In second place is Yaxel Lendeborg, the No. 11 pick, putting up about 22 points and 9 rebounds. Ten picks went by before someone took him. And Nikola Jokić, arguably the best player of the last decade, went 41st. So how good are these teams at this? Two researchers, one a future Nobel Prize winner, looked at the numbers and reached a strange conclusion. The top pick might be a curse.

**1. THE LOSER'S CURSE**
Cade Massey and Richard Thaler studied the NFL draft, every pick and every trade since 1988. The worst team gets the first pick. It's supposed to be a reward for losing. Massey and Thaler argued that it's often the opposite. Teams pay enormous prices to move up, and top picks cost a lot, but the players don't return that value compared with picks a little lower down. They concluded the right to pick first can be more of a curse than a treasure. So why would smart, well-paid experts keep making that mistake? The paper points to four very human errors.

**2. FOUR WAYS TO FOOL YOURSELF**
One: non-regressive predictions. We see an extreme college season and predict an equally extreme future. But extreme performances tend to drift back toward average. Two: overconfidence. The more information experts get, the more confident they feel, and that confidence grows faster than their accuracy. A team with a hundred hours of film feels certain. Three: the winner's curse. If ten teams are bidding on a player, who gets him? The team that overrated him the most. Winning the bid is evidence you paid too much. Four: false consensus. You assume everyone else sees the player the same way you do, so you panic and trade up, when nobody else was going to take him anyway.

**3. BUT WAIT**
Now, I said NFL. Basketball's different. There are five players on the court, not 22, so one superstar can change everything. And a 2026 paper pushed back on Massey and Thaler. It argued that general managers might not be trying to get the best average value. They might be swinging for the rare, franchise-changing player, and a lot of misses might be a rational price for that. That's a fair point. Wembanyama was worth a top pick. So was LeBron. Keep the curse in mind, but don't overdo it. Jokić going 41st doesn't prove the system's useless. It proves it can miss by a lot.

**4. YOU DO THIS TOO**
And before we laugh at general managers, look at us. We've seen a handful of games of these rookies, and fans are already calling people busts and steals. That's non-regressive prediction, live. The Lendeborg numbers are exciting, but they're a tiny sample. So are Dybantsa's. Most of these players are barely out of their teens, with millions of people judging their first week at work. Ask me in three years.

**OUTRO**
Here's what I'd take from this. It isn't just about the draft. It's about hiring, dating, picking a major. The more you know about someone, the more certain you feel. But certainty and accuracy aren't the same thing. So who's the next 41st pick? Name your steal of this draft, and we'll come back in three years to see who was right.
