# P08: How do you blow a 29-point lead? (Cavs vs. Sixers, opening night)

- **Category:** NBA, current season
- **Concepts:** loss aversion, "playing not to lose," ironic process theory, the momentum myth
- **Target length:** ~3:30 (~540 words) · regular video · **film soon**: this is timely
- **Title:** The psychology of blowing a 29-point lead
- **Thumbnail text:** "UP 29. LOST."

**COLD OPEN**
October 22nd, 2026. Opening night in Philadelphia. LeBron James's first home game as a Sixer. The Cleveland Cavaliers go up by 29 points. Twenty-nine. Then they lose, 114 to 106. Philly closes on a 15–0 run in the fourth quarter. It was the biggest comeback of LeBron's entire career, and he's been in the league since 2003. The Cavs had never lost a game after leading by that much. So what actually happens inside a team's head when a lead like that melts?

**1. FROM "WIN" TO "DON'T LOSE"**
Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that losses hurt more than equal gains feel good. Losing $100 stings about twice as much as finding $100 feels nice. Now think about a team up 29. They're not chasing anything anymore. They're protecting something. The goal quietly flips from "go win this" to "don't be the team that blows this." And people in "don't lose" mode tend to play safer and slower, and get more anxious about every mistake. That's not a strategy, it's a mood.

**2. TRYING NOT TO MESS UP CAN MAKE YOU MESS UP**
Psychologist Daniel Wegner studied "ironic processes": trying hard *not* to think of something makes you think of it more. In one study, golfers told to putt and specifically *not* overshoot the hole, while distracted, overshot it more often. So imagine a coach in a timeout yelling "Stop turning it over!" Cleveland finished with 21 turnovers and shot 7 for 29 from three. I can't read their minds, and some of it was just Tyrese Maxey and the Sixers' defense being great. But it's exactly what this research would predict.

**3. WAIT, ISN'T IT JUST MOMENTUM?**
Everybody on TV says "momentum." Here's what the data says. Researchers at Ben-Gurion University looked at almost 900 NBA games where a team came back from behind to force overtime. If momentum is a real force, the comeback team should win overtime more often. They didn't. Home court and team quality mattered. "Momentum" didn't. A follow-up study found sports fans believe in momentum even more strongly than non-fans do. So momentum might be less of a physical force and more of a story we tell after the fact.

**4. THEN WHY DO COLLAPSES FEEL REAL?**
Because the story inside the players' heads is real, even if "momentum" isn't. A lead going from 29 to 15 to 6 feels like falling, and that feeling changes decisions: rushed possessions, hero-ball, passes thrown out of fear. The scoreboard didn't cause the collapse. What the players believed the scoreboard *meant* did.

**OUTRO**
You don't need to be an NBA player for this. Ever been up big in a game, a class, a relationship, and started playing scared? The fix the research points to is boring but real: keep focusing on the next play, not the lead. Play like you're tied. The Cavs will be fine; it's game one of 82. But if you want to know how good teams lose, it usually starts the moment they stop trying to win. Comment the worst collapse you've ever watched.
