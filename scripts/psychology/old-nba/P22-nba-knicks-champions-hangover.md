# P22: The Knicks waited 53 years. Then they forgot how to play.

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video · **update their record before filming**
- **Concepts:** arrival fallacy, hedonic adaptation, impact bias, post-Olympic blues, post-reward resetting
- **Target length:** ~3:45 (~570 words)
- **Title:** Why getting everything you wanted can feel terrible
- **Thumbnail text:** "NOW WHAT?"

---

**COLD OPEN**
In June, the New York Knicks won their first championship in 53 years. Then the new season started, and they lost their first three games. In NBA history, only two other defending champions had ever started 0-3: the 1970 Celtics and the 2012 Mavericks. Neither one won a single playoff series that year. So what happens to a team, or to a person, the moment they finally get everything they ever wanted?

**1. THE ARRIVAL FALLACY**
Psychologist Tal Ben-Shahar has a name for this trap: the arrival fallacy. It's the belief that once you get there, wherever "there" is, you'll be happy, and you'll stay happy. The degree. The job. The relationship. The ring. So you get there, and for a little while it's incredible. And then one Tuesday you wake up, and you're still you, and you still have to do the laundry.

**2. THE LOTTERY STUDY**
In 1978, Philip Brickman and his colleagues interviewed people who'd won major lotteries. They weren't any happier than their neighbors. They also got less pleasure out of everyday things, like a friend's joke or a good breakfast. Your brain adapts. Researchers Tim Wilson and Dan Gilbert later showed we're consistently bad at predicting this. We overestimate how long a good feeling will last. It's called impact bias. We think the ring will feel like the ring forever.

**3. THE OLYMPIC BLUES**
Athletes have a term for the crash: the post-Olympic blues. In 2018, researchers interviewed a small group of British athletes after the Rio Games, and they described huge highs followed by real lows once they got home. A study of Danish athletes after Tokyo found 27 percent reporting low well-being or moderate to severe depression symptoms. These are small studies, and the researchers treat a lot of this as a normal reaction. But think about it. Your whole life was aimed at one date on a calendar. Then the date passes.

**4. THE COFFEE CARD**
There's also a simple motivational reason. Researchers who studied coffee shop loyalty cards found something called the goal gradient: people speed up as they get close to a reward. Then, right after they earn it, they slow back down. They called that post-reward resetting. A title is the biggest coffee card there is. For years, every practice pointed at it. Then you get your free coffee, and your reward is game one of 82, in October, against a team that's dying to beat you. After their 0-3 start, Knicks coach Mike Brown agreed his team looked lethargic. Jalen Brunson said the group gets comfortable when things look good, and that all of it falls on him.

**5. THE WAY BACK**
[UPDATE BEFORE FILMING: They were 2-3 as of Oct 28.] Then came a win over Detroit, built on focusing on the little things. Then a 19-point comeback in Chicago, with 38 from Brunson. It's a few games, so I wouldn't overread it. But notice what changed. Not the goal. The focus. You can't play for "a championship" in October. You can play for one defensive possession. Ben-Shahar doesn't say stop having goals. He says goals are great. The mistake is expecting the finish line to do the work of making you happy.

**OUTRO**
If you've ever gotten what you wanted and felt weirdly flat, you're not ungrateful. You're not broken. Your brain is built to chase. So when you reach the top of something, don't ask, "Why don't I feel better?" Ask, "What's the next small thing worth caring about?" Comment the thing you thought would fix everything, and what it actually felt like.
