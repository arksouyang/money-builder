# P06: The hot hand was "debunked" for 30 years. Then someone checked the math.

- **Category:** NBA
- **Concepts:** hot hand fallacy, how people misjudge randomness, the "law of small numbers," science correcting itself

- **Title:** Is the "hot hand" real? Psychology got it wrong for 30 years
- **Thumbnail text:** "37 POINTS. 1 QUARTER."

---

**COLD OPEN**
January 23rd, 2015. Klay Thompson walks out for the third quarter against the Sacramento Kings and does something nobody in NBA history had ever done. He scores 37 points. In one quarter. Thirteen shots, thirteen makes, nine of them threes. By the end, his teammates were basically handing him the ball and getting out of the way. Everyone in that building believed the same thing: Klay was hot. And for thirty years, psychologists said that belief was an illusion. Then two economists found a mistake in the math.

**1. THE STUDY THAT "KILLED" THE HOT HAND**
In 1985, three psychologists, Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone and Amos Tversky, tested it. Tversky is half of the duo that basically invented behavioral economics, so this was a big deal. They looked at shooting records from the Philadelphia 76ers, free throws from the Boston Celtics, and a controlled shooting experiment with Cornell's basketball teams. The question was simple: after a player hits a few in a row, is he more likely to hit the next one? Their answer was no. The streaks were just what randomness looks like.

**2. WHY YOUR BRAIN SEES STREAKS**
Here's the thing about randomness: it's streakier than you think. Flip a coin twenty times, and the odds are about three in four that you'll get at least four of the same side in a row. But ask people to write down a "random" sequence and they flip back and forth way too often. We expect random to look balanced, so when we see a streak, we assume something is causing it. That became the "hot hand fallacy." It went into textbooks and economics classes. Coaches who believed in the hot hand got treated like people who believe in horoscopes.

**3. THE PLOT TWIST**
In 2015, economists Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo found a problem. Not with the basketball. With the counting. Try this: flip a coin four times. Look at every flip that comes right after a heads, and check what percent of those were heads too. Do that for thousands of four-flip sequences and average it. It's a fair coin, so you'd expect fifty percent. You get about forty. The coin has no memory. The way you're counting short streaks just quietly tilts the result. And the 1985 study counted exactly that way. So a purely random shooter should have looked a little *cold* in their data. The real players looked about even. Correct for the bias, and they were shooting *better* after makes. Miller and Sanjurjo reran the original data and found a real hot hand. Their paper came out in 2018 in one of the top economics journals.

**4. SO WHO'S RIGHT?**
Gilovich pushed back. He told the New York Times their samples were big enough that the conclusion holds. Other researchers using modern tracking data found something in between: players who feel hot really do shoot a little better, but they also start taking harder shots, which hides the effect. Where things roughly stand now: the hot hand is probably real, just smaller than it feels. Klay's night was real. Your friend hitting two in a row at the rec center? Probably still mostly luck.

**OUTRO**
The lesson isn't "trust your gut" or "your gut is wrong." It's that one of the most famous findings in psychology was knocked over by a counting error, and it took thirty years for anyone to notice. Science corrects itself. Just slower than a Klay Thompson third quarter. So next time your teammate hits three in a row, give him the ball. Just not every time.
