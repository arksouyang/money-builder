# P07: Why NBA players refuse the free throw that works

- **Category:** NBA
- **Concepts:** social image vs. performance, threshold models of conformity, "precarious manhood," choking under pressure
- **Target length:** ~3:45 (~560 words) · regular video
- **Title:** Wilt Chamberlain found the perfect free throw. Then he quit using it.
- **Thumbnail text:** "TOO EMBARRASSING TO WORK"

---

**COLD OPEN**
March 2nd, 1962. Wilt Chamberlain scores 100 points in one NBA game, a record that still stands. Here's the part nobody talks about: 28 of those points were free throws. He went 28 for 32 that night, and Wilt was usually a terrible free throw shooter. His secret? He shot them underhanded. Granny style. The next season, he stopped. He went back to the normal way, and he went back to missing. This video is about why someone would knowingly choose to be worse at their job.

**1. THE SHOT THAT WORKS**
Wilt didn't invent it. He got it from Rick Barry, a Hall of Famer who shot underhanded his entire career and made almost 90 percent of his free throws. That's elite, in any era. Barry's argument is simple: your arms hang straight down naturally, you use both hands so the ball doesn't drift sideways, and the ball drops in softer with more arc. And if you're a seven-footer with big hands and stiff shoulders, the normal free throw is genuinely awkward for your body. The underhand shot fixes that.

**2. WHY PLAYERS WON'T DO IT**
Wilt explained why he quit in his own book. He said shooting that way made him feel silly. Like a "sissy." Decades later, Barry reportedly offered to teach Shaquille O'Neal, one of the worst free throw shooters ever. Shaq's answer, as the story goes: he'd rather shoot zero percent. Teams literally built a strategy around his misses. They called it "Hack-a-Shaq." And still, almost nobody switched.

**3. THE PSYCHOLOGY: NOBODY WANTS TO GO FIRST**
Sociologist Mark Granovetter has a model for this. Everyone has a threshold: how many other people have to do something before you'll do it too. A few people have a threshold of zero; they'll do anything first. Most people need to see lots of others do it. Malcolm Gladwell used this exact model to explain the granny shot. For the underhand free throw, almost every player's threshold is sky-high, so nobody goes first, so nobody's threshold ever gets met. Add what psychologists call "precarious manhood": research finds manhood is seen as something you can lose, so men will pay real costs to avoid looking unmanly. For an NBA player, looking goofy in front of 20,000 people and millions on TV is one of those costs.

**4. THE IRONY: PRESSURE MAKES IT WORSE**
Free throws are the one moment in basketball where the game stops and everyone stares at you. Research on choking, by psychologists like Sian Beilock, finds that skilled athletes under pressure start consciously monitoring movements that are usually automatic, and that's exactly when they break down. So a player afraid of looking weird is already thinking too much about how he looks while he shoots. The fear of embarrassment adds to the very pressure that makes him miss.

**OUTRO**
We all have a granny free throw. The study method that looks nerdy. The question you won't ask in lecture. Going to the gym when you don't know what you're doing. The real question isn't "what works?" It's "what am I willing to look stupid doing?" Wilt Chamberlain picked looking cool over 100-point nights.
