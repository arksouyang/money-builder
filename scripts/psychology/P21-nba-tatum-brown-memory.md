# P21: Tatum and Jaylen Brown might both be telling the truth

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video · **post before Celtics at 76ers, Tue Nov 10**
- **Concepts:** egocentric bias, naive realism, overconfidence in text, ostracism
- **Target length:** ~4:15 (~650 words)
- **Title:** The psychology of the Tatum-Brown feud
- **Thumbnail text:** "WHO'S LYING?"

---

**COLD OPEN**
Nine seasons together. One championship. Then on July 1st, Jaylen Brown got traded to Philadelphia, and he and Jayson Tatum reportedly haven't spoken since. Tatum says the trade caught him off guard. Brown answered that online with one cap emoji. A Celtics broadcaster said Brown demanded a trade. Brown's camp called that a total lie, and Brown says he never did. Everybody online has picked who's lying. I want to suggest something stranger. Maybe nobody is.

**1. THE CHORE STUDY**
In 1979, psychologists Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly asked married couples a simple question. Separately, how much of the household work do you do? Breakfast, shopping, big decisions. When you added up each couple's answers, they routinely came to more than 100 percent. Nobody was lying. There's just an obvious reason it happens. You were there for every single thing you did. You only saw part of what your partner did. Your own effort is easy to remember, so it feels bigger. Ross and Sicoly ran the same idea on 158 college basketball players, and found the same pattern. It's held up. A 2016 study with hundreds of MBA students and other groups found people's claimed shares added up to way over 100 percent, and it got worse as groups got bigger.

**2. "I SEE REALITY. YOU SEE SPIN."**
Lee Ross, a different Ross, later gave this a name: naive realism. Each of us feels like we're seeing the world as it actually is. So when someone disagrees, we don't think, "We have different information." We think, "They must be biased, or lying." Think about how a trade works. Dozens of conversations, with agents, executives and teammates, over months. Tatum saw some of those. Brown saw others. Now, I have no idea what was said in those rooms. Neither do you. But two people could walk away from the same nine years with two honest, completely different stories, and each one would feel like the plain truth.

**3. THE EMOJI PROBLEM**
Then it all moves online. In 2005, Justin Kruger and his colleagues ran five experiments on email. People were sure their tone would come across: sarcasm, humor, anger. In one of them, senders predicted 97 percent of readers would get it right. Only 84 percent did. The reason is the punchline. You hear your own tone in your head, so you assume it's on the page. A cap emoji has no tone at all. It's one tiny hat. Every person who saw it read it in the voice they'd already picked.

**4. THE SILENCE**
Here's the part I find saddest. Psychologist Kipling Williams created a game called Cyberball. You toss a ball online with two other players, and after a while, they stop throwing it to you. That's it. People's sense of belonging, control and self-esteem drops. A meta-analysis of 120 of these studies, with nearly 12,000 people, found a large effect. In one study, it dropped even when people were told they were being ignored by a computer. So not talking isn't nothing. Silence is a message, and whoever receives it fills in the blank, usually with the worst version. Tatum says he has no ill will and he's open to a conversation. Brown says there's no beef, but he's got no plans to talk. Both of those can be true. And the silence keeps writing the story anyway.

**5. WHAT HAPPENS NEXT**
On November 10th, Boston goes to Philadelphia for the first time. Two guys who spent nine years learning each other's games, on the same floor, in front of the whole country. Watch the handshake. It'll tell you more than any emoji.

**OUTRO**
You've had this fight. Same event, two memories, each of you certain. Before you decide that person's lying, try one question: "What did you see that I didn't?" It won't fix everything. It might stop you from losing nine years over a cap emoji. So who do you think is right? Actually, don't tell me. Tell me what you'd say to them.
