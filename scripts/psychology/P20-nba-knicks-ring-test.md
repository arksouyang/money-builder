# P20: The Knicks tricked their own staff with a fake championship ring

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video
- **Concepts:** the ultimatum game, psychological contract breach, why phishing training barely works
- **Target length:** ~4:00 (~620 words)
- **Title:** The cruelest email in NBA history
- **Thumbnail text:** "YOU FAILED"

---

**COLD OPEN**
Imagine your company just won the biggest prize in its history. First one in 53 years. You were there through the bad years. Then an email lands in your inbox: click here for your free championship ring. You click. And you get told you failed. It was a test, and now you have to take a cybersecurity class. According to The Athletic, that's what happened to staff at Madison Square Garden in September, after the Knicks won the title. And one of the most reliable findings in behavioral science explains why that stings: people will give up real money to reject something that feels unfair.

**1. WHAT HAPPENED**
Let's be fair about the facts. The email was a phishing test: a fake scam that companies send to see who clicks. A spokesperson for MSG called it an unfortunate mistake. Then on ring night, the Knicks' assistant coaches reportedly got empty boxes, partly because their rings hadn't been sized yet, and they're expected to get them. So maybe nobody meant any harm. But people were furious anyway. Why? Nobody lost any money. Nobody was owed a ring. The answer starts with a very simple game.

**2. THE ULTIMATUM GAME**
Here's how it works. I get ten dollars. I offer you some of it. If you say yes, we both keep our share. If you say no, we both get nothing. Logically, you should take anything. A dollar is a dollar. But a meta-analysis of 37 papers found that about 16 percent of offers got rejected, and the smaller the share, the more often people said no. People will walk away with zero rather than accept a deal that feels insulting. You may have seen the viral clip of a monkey throwing a cucumber at a researcher over this. Great video. But a 2024 meta-analysis of more than 60,000 observations found no real evidence for that in animals. So we're sticking with humans.

**3. IT WAS NEVER ABOUT THE RING**
So here's my read. A ring isn't jewelry. It's a receipt. It says: you were part of this. That's why an empty box can sting more than no box at all. It's a receipt with nothing on it.

**4. THE CONTRACT NOBODY SIGNED**
Psychologist Denise Rousseau studies something called the psychological contract: the unwritten deal between you and the place you work. Nobody promised these employees a ring. Legally, there was no broken promise. But a phishing test could have been a fake package delivery. This one used the organization's own proudest moment as bait. It took the feeling of "we did this together" and turned it into the trap. A 2007 meta-analysis found that when people feel that deal was broken, they report much lower job satisfaction, lower commitment and more desire to leave. Those studies are correlational, but the pattern is consistent.

**5. DID IT EVEN WORK?**
Here's the best part. In 2022, researchers at ETH Zurich followed nearly 15,000 employees for 15 months. Showing people a lesson right after they fell for a fake email didn't make them safer. Then in 2025, a randomized experiment at UC San Diego Health tested more than 19,500 employees over eight months. That training reduced clicking by just 2 percent. And the lure mattered: under 2 percent clicked a fake password update, but about 31 percent clicked a fake vacation policy update. People click on what they care about. So a free ring was always going to work. It proved that the staff cared.

**OUTRO**
So the people who clicked weren't stupid. They were hopeful. They believed they were part of the team. And if you ever run anything, a club, a shift, a company, remember the ultimatum game. People will accept a small share. They won't accept being insulted. What's the worst thing a job has ever done to you? Tell me in the comments.
