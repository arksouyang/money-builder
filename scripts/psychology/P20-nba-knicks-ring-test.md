# P20: The Knicks tricked their own staff with a fake championship ring

- **Category:** NBA · **Format:** long, regular video
- **Concepts:** equity theory, inequity aversion, psychological contract, why phishing tests can backfire
- **Target length:** ~4:00 (~610 words)
- **Title:** The cruelest email in NBA history (and what a monkey can teach us)
- **Thumbnail text:** "YOU FAILED"

---

**COLD OPEN**
Imagine your company just won the biggest prize in its history. First one in 53 years. You were there through the bad years. Then an email lands in your inbox: click here for your free championship ring. You click. And you get told you failed. It was a test, and now you have to take a cybersecurity class. According to The Athletic, that's what happened to staff at Madison Square Garden in September, after the Knicks won the title. And there's a famous experiment with monkeys that explains exactly why that stings.

**1. WHAT HAPPENED**
Let's be fair about the facts. The email was a phishing test: a fake scam that companies send to see who clicks. A spokesperson for MSG called it an unfortunate mistake. Then on ring night, the Knicks' assistant coaches reportedly got empty boxes, partly because their rings hadn't been sized yet, and they're expected to get them. So maybe nobody meant any harm. But people were furious anyway. Why? Nobody lost any money. Nobody was owed a ring. The answer starts with a cucumber.

**2. THE CUCUMBER EXPERIMENT**
In 2003, Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal trained capuchin monkeys to hand over a small token in exchange for food. A slice of cucumber? Great. Happy to do it. Then they changed one thing. A monkey would watch the monkey next to her get a grape, a much better food, for the same token. Cooperation dropped from about 95 percent to about 60. When the neighbor got a grape for doing nothing at all, it fell to about 20. Some monkeys refused the cucumber. Some threw the token away. Nothing about the cucumber had changed. Only the comparison. It's a small study, just five monkeys, and scientists still argue about whether that's truly fairness or just frustration. But if you've ever found out your coworker makes more than you, you know that feeling.

**3. IT WAS NEVER ABOUT THE RING**
In 1965, psychologist J. Stacy Adams proposed equity theory. We don't judge what we get on its own. We compare what we put in and what we got out against everyone around us. A ring isn't jewelry. It's a receipt. It says: you were part of this. That's why an empty box hurts more than no box. It's a receipt with nothing on it.

**4. THE CONTRACT NOBODY SIGNED**
Psychologist Denise Rousseau studies something called the psychological contract: the unwritten deal between you and the place you work. Nobody promised these employees a ring. Legally, there was no broken promise. But a phishing test could have been a fake package delivery. This one used the organization's own proudest moment as bait. It took the feeling of "we did this together" and turned it into the trap. Research on broken psychological contracts links them to lower trust and lower commitment.

**5. DID IT EVEN WORK?**
Here's the best part. In 2022, researchers at ETH Zurich ran one of the biggest studies on this: nearly 15,000 employees over 15 months. Showing people a lesson right after they fell for a fake email didn't make them safer, and it may have made them more likely to fall for the next one. What helped? Warnings on suspicious emails, and an easy button for reporting them. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has warned that punishing staff over these tests can start to look like entrapment.

**OUTRO**
So the people who clicked weren't stupid. They were hopeful. They believed they were part of the team. And if you ever run anything, a club, a shift, a company, remember the monkey. People can live with a cucumber. They can't live with being told the cucumber was a test. What's the worst thing a job has ever done to you? Tell me in the comments.
