# P16: How politicians dodge questions without you noticing

- **Category:** Politics (**strictly nonpartisan**)
- **Concepts:** the "artful dodger" studies, equivocation research, attention and social evaluation, media-training "bridging"
- **Target length:** ~3:30 (~530 words) · regular video · timely around the midterms
- **Title:** The psychology of the political dodge (and how to catch it)
- **Thumbnail text:** "DID THEY ANSWER?"

---

**COLD OPEN**
Quick test. Think of the last political interview or debate clip you watched. Can you remember the exact question that was asked? Not the topic. The question. Most people can't. And politicians know that. Researchers have measured how often politicians actually answer the question they're asked, and how often you notice when they don't. Both numbers are worse than you'd hope.

**1. HOW OFTEN THEY ANSWER**
British psychologist Peter Bull has spent decades coding political interviews question by question. He's catalogued dozens of distinct ways politicians avoid answering. Across studies of televised interviews in the UK, politicians gave a clear, direct reply to fewer than four in ten questions. In one Japanese study, it was under one in ten. This isn't one party or one country. It's the job.

**2. THE ARTFUL DODGER EXPERIMENT**
In 2011, Harvard researchers Todd Rogers and Michael Norton ran an experiment. People watched a video of a speaker being asked a policy question in a mock debate. In one version, he answered it. In another, he smoothly answered a *different* question, on a related topic, as if it were the one he'd been asked. A lot of viewers didn't notice the dodge. And the ones who didn't notice didn't think any less of him. When the answer was about something totally unrelated, people caught it. But a dodge to a *similar* question slid right past.

**3. WHY YOUR BRAIN MISSES IT**
Rogers and Norton's explanation is my favorite part. When we watch someone talk, our brains aren't really asking, "Did he answer the question?" They're asking, "Do I like this person? Does he seem trustworthy?" We're judging the person, not checking the content. In their study, a speaker who answered the right question but stumbled over his words came off *worse* than a smooth speaker who dodged. Confidence beats correctness.

**4. THE TECHNIQUE HAS A NAME**
Media trainers teach this on purpose. It's often called "bridging": acknowledge the question, use a bridge phrase, then pivot to your talking point. "That's a great question, and what Americans really care about is..." Once you know the bridge phrases, you'll hear them everywhere: "Let me be clear," "The real issue here is," "What I'd say is."

**5. THE FIX IS ALMOST TOO EASY**
In the same research, one small change made viewers much better at spotting dodges: keeping the question written on screen while the person answered. That's it. When the question stays in front of you, your brain stops just judging the person and starts checking the answer. Some news networks have started doing exactly that.

**OUTRO**
So here's your homework for election week. Next time you watch an interview or debate, write the question down, or just say it out loud before the answer starts. Then ask: did they answer *that*? It works on every politician, from every side. Comment the most impressive dodge you've ever seen. Not the worst. The most *impressive*.
